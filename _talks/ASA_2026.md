Low-luminosity Type 2 active galactic nuclei (AGN) are often undetected in X-ray surveys, despite exhibiting unambiguous AGN signatures at optical and infrared wavelengths. This apparent inconsistency challenges the standard orientation-based AGN model. We analyse the origin of X-ray non-detections using an optical sample of 798 Type 1 and 2607 Type 2 AGN, cross-matched with eROSITA and WISE. We demonstrate that mid-infrared colour--colour diagram selects more luminous Type 2 AGN, introducing a strong luminosity bias. Multiwavelength analysis reveals that X-ray non-detections are intrinsically X-ray-weak and not driven by eROSITA's sensitivity limits. The absence of strong X-ray emission points to suppression or disappearance of the corona rather than simple obscuration. We propose a physically motivated disk–wind framework in which a radiatively driven wind launched from the accretion disk sustains the corona, broad-line region (BLR), and dusty structures. In this picture, declining accretion rates quench the corona on short timescales, eliminating X-ray emission, while larger-scale structures, such as the narrow-line region (NLR), persist. Our results show that time-dependent accretion and a disk-wind play pivotal roles in shaping AGN classification beyond orientation-based unification.
